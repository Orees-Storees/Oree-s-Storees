[13.15.26] We can't control the rain

Weather by Jenny Offill

Inside the sleeve:

Inside the sleeve: Lizzie works in the library of a university where she was once a promising, graduate student. Her side hustle is answering the letters that come in to “Hell and High Water”, the doom-laden podcast hosted by her former mentor. At first it suits her, this chance to practice her other calling as an unofficial shrink. She has always played this role to her divorced mother and recovering-addict brother. But everyone who writes in to the show is either crazy or depressed, and soon Lizzie finds herself struggling to strike the obligatory note of hope in her responses. The reassuring rhythms of her life as a wife and mother begin to falter as her obsession with disaster psychology and people preparing for the end of the world grows.

I am going to say this off the bat, this commentary is coherent-ish and choppy. There’s something about the way this entry and the Bonjour one are written that irritate me. I don’t really know how to fix them, don’t have an interest to, am bored with the way I talk about things and can’t quite put my finger on the what I mean (at least with this book). Don't even get me started about the grammar, ok. … I’ve decided to add emojis to reduce my anger. Enjoy where you can.

“What is the core of delusion?”

There’s really no better way to describe Weather besides the witnessing of the most relevant moments in a day that one remembers. These events perpetuate in us a disposition about our reality. This including, not limited to: interactions with a neighbor or being reassured by a loved one, feeling the collective energy of the city one is in or the enduring pangs of pain one feels in that same part of their body daily. These internal things that infuse with the external things creating “perspective”. Weather is really well written in the way that we quietly and very normally watch Lizzie go about her days. The matter of her slipping into a dooming mindset isn’t abrupt, but instead a snowball rolling down a hill. We, the audience, witness Lizzie’s life across two-ish years. This compounding of many things at once: the questions people send in from her mentor’s podcast, her supporting her recovering-brother, the election [which we can understand as the begining of the United State’s current administration] and, of course, the ever looming knowledge of the climate crisis. All these things and other things just piling up and Lizzie’s trying to seek resolutions to these matters somehow, but she can’t.

This collective post-election hysteria that contributes to Lizzie’s deterioration is a factor I specifically want to talk about, briefly. I really love the consideration Offill had when talking about this matter in relation to race/ethnicity. It wasn’t often, but clearly, observantly intentioned when mentioned. Lizzie’s neighborhood, that’s also endearingly nicknamed “Little Pakistan” is utilized as a constant external measure that aids to soft focus on the hypocrisy of these crises. Lizzie and her family live in Flatbush, which I am decently familiar with. It’s kind of funny because Flatbush is the current gentrification zone in Brooklyn, you know, and then you have all these white people freaking about the current admin while displacing/putting heat (in regards to hate) onto generational non-white residences. These conflicting matters of crises is so delicately made apparent in Offill’s prose in which she’s not necessarily making a point about it, but allows it space to be recognized through Lizzie as this matter of perspective.

Funny enough, Offill doesn’t ever mention black and I can understand why. Talking blackness for white writers is sticky and usually, immediately pinholes conversation. Inferring black people, in this case by simply knowing the neighborhood, and discussing the other ethnic groups that live in the area, allows the conversation to happen without any unnecessary thought derailments. I don't really have any interest or whatever talking about the political stressors of this book which is pretty telling in its own way. There's even a part where Lizzie is talking with an Iranian character and pushes them to state their opinion about America's political hooplah after they try to brush off the convo. Obviously not able to change subjects until Lizzie is satisfied, the character says: "what's happened to everyone else is, now happening to your people". I was like "yea". That's the distress/hypocrisy that Offill is displaying. I can see arguments for Offill as insincere because she is using ethnic characters in that storytelling way to talk through them or around them or whatever, but isn't that how a story works? lol. Trying to call that out adds to it, specifically within this moment, as a functional ironic/meta element. People are entitled to their own opinion, yk. I can comfortably say, in the way in which this book is written, Offill is not ignorant of herself as a common-day white person. Anyways…

Some of the other characters 𖠋𖠋𖠋:

Ben: The love Lizzie and her husband, Ben, share was like a lamp post in the dark. She’d be dooming and his kindness was just enough to keep her head above the water. Of course, he is only human and needs to sustain himself also, but he handled his weariness much differently than Lizzie. Lizzie would let herself wallow in it, not like in a pitying way, but a searching of answers that just made her loop. Ben “would do the math”, assess what he could in front of him and carry on, get away, take a break, put up a quote. He doesn’t entertain her dooming, but tries to just keep his own facts straight as much as possible kindly and sweetly.

Sylvia: Being the leader of hope is an arduous task that requires everyone to carry their weight. I don’t blame Lizzie’s mentor and podcast host, Sylvia, for stepping away from the work of being this hope rationalizer in the middle of the book. Sylvia often relied on dismantling human superiority and making connections to human’s primitive functions as a means of helping people articulate how we got to where we are now because of this complex. To be frank, this is it and not it at the same time. I feel like there is a better way to describe how she dispenses hope, but this is the only way I can wrap my brain around it. I don’t fully disagree with this practice, at least in the matter of human sensorial mediocrity, but 1) I think humans are distinctly unique animals and 2) I don’t agree with the practice of garnering hope in the form of intellectualized logic because that just seeks more answers. I feel I often witness hope coming from things of dreams, faith, matters that defy perceived/logical/scientific possibility. Hope is not a matter that is tangible with our own eyes and biology. Witnessing hope within the past, present and future is outside of all of these things. I think this is why she gets consumed by the void with the podcast and chooses to end the project all together. She can’t logically answer all the questions.

Will: Out of all the characters, there’s one I am really trying to understand and that’s Will. This stranger, learned to be wartime reporter, that Lizzie meets up with regularly while Ben and her son, Eli are out of town camping for three weeks. From their initial meeting on the public bus, their dynamic was of a crush type. He was interested in her and she was interested in him. They keep running into each other around the neighborhood and decide to grab a drink. Will and Lizzie never go any farther than public conversation (we can call them dates) and texting each other, as couth as one can be under these circumstances. She doesn’t tell Will about her husband and son and they are cordial with one another. My personal opinion on this brief relationship is tricky. I am really uncomfortable with her skirting this physical line considering her attraction to Will (dare I say, it is infidelity), and I also believe that the grey area of a dynamic doesn’t need to be immediately marked and labelled because of its ill-existence to which we don’t explore it/choose to disregard it. That’s close enough to me verbalizing that sentiment... I guess I am trying to rationalize the lesson Will was for Lizzie.

It seemed Will was this realistic danger in the way in which he is an attractive man, attuned and curious of her, along with her doomer survival knowledge, that provides Lizzie the necessary space to articulate/reawaken her love and appreciation for her husband. This love was never lost, but deeply muddled by all the bullshit. This stable rock that she has that is her marriage to someone she loves. Maybe Offill is setting up this matter of “flirtation” of a potential actual crisis for Lizzie. This dooming leading to emotional infidelity to physical infidelity. We witness this as a speedrun with her brother who, in his immense anxiety of killing his newborn daughter, ends up relapsing and cheating on his wife (of one year) with an ex. Lizzie is so worried about this and that when her lack of hope is setting herself up to make a very poor decision because of that “why the hell not” factor. We see her bounce around with this which ultimately results in her not sleeping with Will, but tethering that line enough to where it’s “not cheating”.

I find solace in my own conclusion that Ben “finds out” about Lizzie’s emotional infidelity when he discovers cigarettes in a drawer and questions when Lizzie picked up smoking. It’s never metioned if they had a conversation, but simply understood in the way that Ben “did the math, all the math.” To which he puts up an Epictetus quote that states: “You are not some disinterested bystander/Exert yourself.” What the freak does that mean? I don’t know, but I think it means he understands his wife was thinking/doing inconsiderate things to him while he and their son was away.

Seeing Lizzie come out of the hole is just as quiet as her entering it. The hill stops eventually and the snowball looses momentum. The ball sits for a day or two and as the weather warms up, or simply doesn't stay cold enough to sustain it, its melts. I don’t know the way in which Lizzie makes peace, she just does. This being in forms of trying out things she used to be rigid to: praying, going to church with her mom, taking care of medical things she’s been hesitant about, appreciating the way in which light filters a space, being comforted by her husband (in the way that they plan a doomstead/at night how he consoles her of noises on their roof). There’s a whole continuous plot line with this meditation class that ends up really aiding her and her brother, but I don’t have the stamina to get into that. I guess it’s just that matter of life where something starts as a drop of interaction and then turns into a sand moving wave or whatever. All we really know is that Lizzie becomes more comfortable encompassing the present to the best of her ability. All in all, this leads to the summing up of all her anxiety and other future based thinking within the last sentence of the book: “The core of delusion is that I am here and you are there.”

☔︎︎ ⋆｡ﾟ☁︎｡⋆ ⛆

I’ve liked this book the most out of everything I’ve read all year. I like the way the prose is written and the way the font changes depending on what’s written; whether it's a thought, a quote, a flyer, an email, etc. Jenny Offill keeps it fun and easy, yet doesn’t relinquish being cryptic or not showing her full hand. I think this book is relatable in the way in which we all are made unstable by all the crazy ass things outside of us that we cannot control and so desperately want to. I conclude this commentary with a link that was provided at the end of the book. Check it out. It has good tips. And if you want read Weather. Its an inoffensive and intriguing read.
