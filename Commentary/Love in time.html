[26.10.26] The Matter of Circumstance and Divine Timing

Love In The Time of Cholera by Gabriel García Márquez

From the Back:

In their youth, Florentino Ariza and Fermina Daza fall passionately in love. When Fermina eventually chooses to marry a wealthy, well-born doctor, Florentino is devastated, but he is a romantic. As he rises in his business career he whiles away the years in 622 affairs – yet he reserves his heart for Fermina. Her husdand dies at last, and Florentino purposefully attends the funeral. Fifty-one years, nine months and four days after he first declared his love for Fermina, he will do so again.

I randomly chose to read Love in the Time of Cholera because I thought it could teach me something about love. Specifically “what (the hell) is love”. This came at a time over the two middle weeks of October where much introspecting (reverse engineering) was to be had on matters I value [these matters being love]. This work ultimately sought to dissect love from the curfuffle that is: sex as primal activity (foremost) that scratches many itches, fucking, making love, intimacy & sensuality, dedicated energetic application, gratification/satisfaction and modern emptiness. I felt reading this book would help me understand what I was obsessively appealed to as a middle and high schooler in the many many works of yaoi I read before taking the leap myself at the age of 18 with my first and only relationship. This bridging of mental creation made tangible in the act of physical creation, not lasting, but ultimately resulting in a seeking (aka the emptiness) that, in my sparse engagements (partners) over the years since the end of my relationship, only brought about long heartache post the much more transient “satisfaction”. No pain, no gain. The few flings and many days of thinking afterward ultimately led to the greatest lesson of my life thus far: I am incapable of separating sex and love and must move in accordance with that [which is for the better, honestly]. So as I bring things back around since I have more than enough time to just think currently, I sought to at least understand what little virgin Oree saw and adored in the copious amounts of porn she read, haha. What better a source than a popular romance.

I acquired this book when I was on the hunt for reading material for a lesson plan while posting up “call for work” flyers around my hometown cities. It was a used bookstore and I just perused it like any casual shopper. “Love in the Time of Cholera” caught my eye on the shelf, to which I recalled my bestie telling me “it’s sad”. I picked it up, read a random page, put it back and left the store to grab a beer. I play games of chance with things I’m uncertain about. If anything is still on my mind, I go back. If not then matters write themselves. From the time of getting in my car and driving 15 mins away, I hadn’t stopped thinking about the book, so I hit a U-turn, returned to the store, left with it in hand and started reading it at my beer destination. I read about 5 pages and then neglected the book for a day or two as I recognized my obsessive nature immediately: as soon as I hit a certain point (aka Florentino Ariza being introduced) I wasn’t going to be able to do anything else except for read until it was over. This was a matter of being under arrest. I wanted to do other things and couldn’t. At traffic lights I would read. Before and after my errand location I would read. My family would call me to dinner and I would tell them to leave me alone to read. It was obsessive and not in a way that was pleasurable. It was 4 days of desperation.

--

I’m not really interested in coherently recapping the book, so I’ll establish the characters. The story has 3 main characters: Florentino Ariza (the lover), Fermina Daza (the beloved) and Dr. Juvenal Urbino de la Calle (the husband). Pretty soon into the book, its possible to decipher each of these character’s disposition to their world and how this makes them think and move in relativity to time. This is especially the case for Fermina Daza who is a beautiful woman alive in late 19th century Colombia; the object of love, a social material and the gendered matter craved/least cared about/hated all at the same time. We understand clear the motives of the men, that of which is to move with social order in which they as men had a lot more mobility to do within these restricted dynamics about and toward women.

I established Florentino Ariza as a venusian in the way he loves and sees the beauty of everything. A pink tinge in his perspective passion that moves him accordingly, particularly in his attachment to the past. To have passion is to suffer and I think there is something interesting there. A topic to dig into later. Dr. Juvenal Urbino is a realist. This being most notable in his high class standing; the utmost alignment in the most influential way of all hierarchal systems (work, lineage and religion). This realist disposition makes him a cowardly man. He lacks all the whimsey that allows Florentino Ariza to float through the world in this somnambulant state, but he utilizes this fear to produce a lot of necessary change in the world that exists around him. Arguably, Florentino Ariza does the same too with his love. I think whats interesting about these character's contrasts is that it leads them back to similar conclusions in ways, at least in the matters of nostalgia and how they treat women they can sex.

When it came to Fermina Daza, specifically at the ripe age to wed in which she is neither interested in Florentino Ariza or Dr. Juvenal Urbino, I was like “well, what does she want?” She’s read as a beautiful, incredibly stubborn puzzle until Márquez provides her insight halfway into the book. Fermina Daza is not afforded the past in the same way that the two male leads can interact with it. She can’t reflect on it because after her adolescence, where she gets this brief engagement with her own freewill post being under her father’s strict watch, she’s expected to marry. Reflection brings her grief because of her lack of autonomy. Initially, I considered her the cynic, but as I came to terms with her being at peace with her life’s choices and overall her lack of love for Florentino Ariza until old age, I now consider her to be the pragmatic. She’s not out of touch with ambition and passion, but as her’s doesn’t correlate with not being able to have a man socially, she resigns herself to her womanly task aka marrying someone who sets her up for life and bearing kids.

The one thing I can say I learned clearly about love from this book is that love is an absolute spiritual conviction. That’s the passion. That’s the fire. Every time Florentino Ariza spoke clearly and confidently of his love for Fermina Daza, it was understood by everyone that he was “speaking from the Holy Spirit”. This absolute conviction that no one could deter him from and that he never once questioned no matter what he was faced with. I liked that he was wise about keeping his business to himself unless it was required for him to state it, which it usually wasn’t. He’s never taken as a sloppy lover, but instead he is someone who is calculating, observant and careful (at least in his youth). I liked how in their youth when they initially fall in love, Fermina Daza and Florentino Ariza’s compatibility is understood within their composure and will. They were teens, but they carried themselves very put together. I don’t talk about it at all in this review, but letter writing plays a big role in the book, particularly in the way that Florentino Ariza is a very talented love writer. Reminds me of a time I gave a stranger a scented note with my phone number and it was kind of thrilling. Gotta tap into that more.

As lovely as Florentio Ariza’s love sounds, I kind of don’t know what to do with his “making do” of life as he holds strong to his spiritual assignment to Fermina Daza. Just cause you can sleep with a lot of people (and you know we love a horny man – under respectful and consenting circumstances lol) should you? In the book, its understood that this secular activity is a space-filler for his true love. This discovery occuring after he was raped (a revoking of his virginity) by an anon, mind you. [There’s something curious about the ripping away of his virginity by a rando since he was holding out for love, aka Fermina Daza, but I don’t feel like continuing this thread out of laziness.] Through these affairs Florentio Ariza comes to understand that he can love multiple people at one time. I take this genuinely, yet I argue and he would maybe agree, that it isn’t love because the fire is simply physical. It’s not scratching that spiritual itch. He’s never satisfied with just the sex. This is especially the case after he achieves 50 where he becomes increasingly careless. It became a matter of him getting his dick wet. His love was always reckless, but this is magnified in his old age to the point that the intentioned, narrative words of “love” and this that aren’t able to camouflage his increasingly amoral escapades. There becomes a clear perversion of whatever “love” is onto his affairs so that he can sleep at night. He and Dr. Juvenal Urbino did amoral shit in this regard. Why is that?

I found peace with two things in the book: Fermina Daza never regretted anything she did and she and Florentino Ariza had to respectively live and learn in order to be able to love in their special ordained way. The love was only meant to manifest at old age. If you dwell on the time that has passed without its achievement, you only feel forlorn. Maybe that's one of the points. In a youthful cynicism and doomer fashion, I wrote a particular note back in ‘24 when I first moved to nyc. I stated: “A young man transforming into an old man is just the proces of aging. A young woman transforming into an old woman is a loss of woman-ness." ... Jokes on me though because being a woman past 50 liberates her from the linearity of many systems. Her specifically female functionally at the end of its line to where she can just “be”. Towards the end of her life, Fermina Daza was finally able to do what she wanted to do and Florentino Ariza learned the proper way to communicate to her. That took their lives and a technology, the typewriter, that didn’t even exist for 2/3 of it. Necessary things outside of them had to occur first for them to be together. Truly process-based thinking instead of destination-based thinking.

Anyways, what truly kept me going was just how exquisite Márquez depicts the tropical climate and materiality of interiors. Wow. This book had me longing to be in these humid light filled spaces with amazingly crafted furniture and scents. This is literally my dream to live in an exotically crafted home. Every room filled with art and fine craftsmanship of irreplaceable items, hosting amazing parties and dinners, sitting on balconies smoking/enjoying, all the windows open with every opportunity of natural light captured within every detail of the architecture. Maybe what disturbed me the most was the passing of time because I am well aware of what follows that period: Material Degradation. This is a similar feeling of awe and lament over erasure that I felt after watching Daughters of the Dust. You can only hold an appreciation for what once was without grievance or else you just end up sad.

This commentary gets no conclusion. Read the book. Watch the movie. Don’t do either. Up to you.
